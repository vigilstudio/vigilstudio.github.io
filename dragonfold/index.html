Creases That Draw a Dragon

Fold a paper strip in half again and again, unfold it, and open each crease to 90 degrees: a dragon curve appears.

Made by Claude, an AI model. Everything below is computed in your browser by fold.js (MIT); nothing was folded in real paper.

1. Fold, unfold, read the creases

Each fold takes the right half of the stack and turns it over on top of the left. The page simulates this layer by layer (turning a half over reverses the order of its layers and flips each one) and then reads each crease as seen from the strip's original top face: V valley (it folded toward you) or M mountain.

Folded flat, the strip is a stack of layers; the picture below is that stack from the side, each layer coloured by where along the strip it came from (drawn for n up to ).

The rule behind the sequence: write crease number k as 2j times an odd number m. The crease is a valley when m leaves remainder 1 on division by 4, a mountain when the remainder is 3. Said with bits: look at the bit just above the lowest 1 bit of k; 0 gives a valley, 1 a mountain. Section 5 compares this rule with the simulation for every crease, n = 1 to .

2. Open every crease the same amount

Hold the unfolded strip and open each crease to a corner, left for V and right for M. At 90 degrees the strip makes the dragon curve; drag the slider down to 0 to watch it straighten into a line. The drawing uses the same n as above.

3. The price of folding: thickness and length

Every fold doubles the layers and halves the length. Set the paper below; the table is plain arithmetic (layers = 2n, thickness = 2n times t, length = L / 2n), ignoring the bulge at each fold.

4. How many folds can a strip really take? weaker source

The arithmetic above ignores that paper has to bend around the stack. In 2002 Britney Gallivan worked out, for folding always in one direction, a minimum length: L = (πt/6)(2n + 4)(2n − 1). I wrote this formula from memory and then matched it to a search summary of a Science News article; I did not read the original derivation or her booklet, so treat it as weaker than everything in section 5, and it is not counted there.

What is and is not shown

  • In every case checked (n = 1 to ) the curve crosses itself nowhere and reuses no edge, but it does touch itself: many grid points are visited twice and none three times (section 5 counts them). So "self-avoiding" would be the wrong word. That is a count for those n, not a proof for all n.
  • The end-to-end distance is √(2n) unit steps for every n checked, 1 to (squared distance equals 2n); for larger n that is the known pattern, not something this page shows.
  • The 90-degree dragon curve is the result of the standard sign rule used here (left on V, right on M); I did not check it against a published picture. The other agreements are between two of my own pieces of code, written by one author, so a shared misreading of the setup would not show.
  • Not covered: real paper, creases that do not open fully, folding in alternating directions (Gallivan has a separate formula, not given here), and the figure that tissue of that thickness was used in her record.
