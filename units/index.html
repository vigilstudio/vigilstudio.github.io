Why a Mile Is 5,280 Feet

A mile is 5,280 feet because eight 660-foot furlongs were fixed to a mile; the inch became exactly 2.54 cm in 1959.

Why 5,280

The number is not a design, it is a product of three older measures that each made sense on their own: the rod (16.5 feet, a pole to prod oxen), the furlong (a furrow-length, 40 rods) and the mile (8 furlongs). Multiply and you get the figure; the page does it from the ladder above:

Count by yards and the same mile is 1,760; count by inches, 63,360. Each of those routes is typed in again by hand in the self-checks below, separately from the ladder, so a slip in any one shows up as a disagreement.

History, with its sources. I did not look these up.

WhenWhatStatus
Roman timesThe word "mile" comes from mille passus, a thousand paces, which is why the old mile was about a thousand double steps.from memory
1593An English statute of Elizabeth I is usually quoted as fixing the mile at eight furlongs, so that the land measures (furlong, rod, chain) and the road mile agreed at 5,280 feet.from memory
1866 and 1893US law tied the yard to the metre as 3600/3937 m (1 m = 39.37 inches); the 1893 Mendenhall Order made the metre the base.from memory
1929An international conference adopted the nautical mile as exactly 1852 m. The page's check below shows why 1852: it is the minute of arc on a 40,000 km meridian, rounded.from memory (the rounding is checked)
1959The US federal notice kept the survey foot at 1200/3937 m for surveying (this part appeared in a search summary of a NC GIS slide quoting the notice). The same year, the US, UK and other Commonwealth countries agreed the yard is 0.9144 m exactly, hence the inch is 2.54 cm.survey foot: search summary; the six-country agreement: from memory
2022The US survey foot was retired at the end of 2022 in favour of the international foot.from memory

What is not shown here

  • The history above is mostly from memory; the arithmetic is what the page checks, not the dates.
  • The second list is a set of search-summary figures, not the NIST pages or Handbook 44 themselves, which could not be fetched here.
  • The page covers lengths only: no areas (the acre), weights or volumes.
